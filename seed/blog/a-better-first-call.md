I still take a share of our first calls. Not because nobody else can, but because it is the fastest way to find out what people actually want from us, and because I like them. I have done enough of them by now that I can tell in the first two minutes which kind of call it is going to be.

The good ones have something in common, and it is not the prospect. It is that I knew, before I joined, what they were trying to fix. The wasted ones are the ones where the first ten minutes are spent finding that out. The difference was decided on the booking form.

## Ask three questions, not ten

Every question on a booking form costs you a few bookings. People abandon long forms, and the ones who finish arrive slightly annoyed. So the questions have to earn their place. Three do:

- What are you trying to fix? Free text, one line. The answer tells you what the call is about in the prospect's own words, which are the words you should use on the call.
- How many people would use this? A number. It tells you which plan to talk about and whether the person booking is the buyer or the scout.
- When do you need it working? A rough date. It tells you whether this is a call to help or a call to close, and those are different calls.

Skip the ones you can find out yourself. The company is in the email domain. The job title is a search away. Every question the prospect could reasonably think you should already know the answer to is a question that makes the form feel like a screening.

## Name the meeting honestly

The event type is the first thing the prospect reads, and most of them are named for the seller. Discovery call. Strategy session. A prospect does not want to be discovered. They want to know whether the thing works and what it costs.

Name the meeting for what will happen: 30 min intro call. Then use the description to say what will happen in it. Two sentences, what you will ask and what they will get. We will go through what you are scheduling today and show you how a team page would handle it. You will leave with a working page or a clear reason this is not for you. The second half of that sentence gets more bookings than any promise would, because it sounds like a person wrote it.

## Let the answers change the call

Read the answers before the call, not on it. This sounds obvious and is skipped constantly. If the prospect wrote that their recruiters spend half their day emailing candidates about times, the call should open with recruiting, not with a walkthrough of the product from the top.

If your team is big enough that different people should take different calls, let the form do the sorting. On the Team plan, a routing form asks the questions before the booking page and sends the prospect to the right one: bigger teams to a round-robin page of account executives, small ones to a page for a shorter call, or straight to the [signup page](/signup) if that is honestly the better next step. The prospect never sees the sorting. They see a calendar that fits.

## Confirm what you will do with the time

The confirmation email is read more carefully than anything else you send, because the prospect has just committed half an hour to you. Use it. Repeat what they told you, in their words. Say what you will bring. Ask for the one thing that would make the call better, whether that is the colleague who owns the calendar today or a link to the page where bookings currently happen.

And make sure the reminder before the call carries a reschedule link. A prospect who has to move the call and cannot easily do it does not move it. They miss it, and then they are embarrassed, and then they are gone.

## The call itself

By the time you join, you know what they want, how many people it is for, and when they need it, and you have already said what the half hour is for. The first two minutes are spent confirming that, and the other twenty-eight on the actual problem. That is the whole trick. The form did the work.

## Checklist

- Ask three questions: what they are fixing, how many people, and when.
- Drop any question you could answer yourself.
- Name the event type for what will happen and how long it takes.
- Write a two-sentence description with what you will ask and what they will get.
- Read the answers before the call.
- If different calls need different people, use a routing form to sort them.
- Send a confirmation that repeats their words and says what you will bring.
- Keep a reschedule link in every reminder.
