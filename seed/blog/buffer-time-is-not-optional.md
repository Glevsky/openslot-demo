A calendar with no gaps looks like a productive day. In practice it is a late one. The 10:00 runs to 10:34, the 10:30 starts four minutes behind, and by 3:00 the person you are meeting has been waiting in a lobby, virtual or otherwise, for a quarter of an hour.

I look at a lot of booking data in my job, and the pattern is consistent enough that I want to be blunt about it: buffer time is not a nice-to-have. It is the setting that decides whether the rest of your availability rules work at all.

## What a buffer actually does

A buffer is a gap that the booking page keeps clear before or after every meeting. Ten minutes after a call means nobody can book you at 10:30 if you are on a call until 10:30. That is all it is. The effect is bigger than the mechanism.

The gap absorbs the overrun. It gives you a place to write down the two things you promised to send. It means you walk into the next meeting knowing whose name is on the invite. And, for the person on the other end, it means the meeting starts when the confirmation email said it would. A buffer before a meeting works the same way in the other direction, and is worth setting on anything you need to prepare for.

## The three rules that do most of the work

### A buffer after every meeting

Ten minutes is the floor. Fifteen is better for anything involving a customer. If you regularly need more than that, the meeting is probably too short, which is a different problem.

### A daily limit

The number of external meetings you can do well in a day is smaller than the number you can fit. Most people who set a daily limit for the first time set it too high and lower it within a month. Start with the number you would choose if every one of those calls was important, because on a booking page, every one of them is.

### Minimum notice

Requiring a few hours of notice, or a day for anything that needs preparation, means you never see a new meeting on your calendar with 20 minutes to spare. It also keeps buffers honest. A same-day booking has a way of landing exactly where you were going to catch up.

## What we see in the numbers

We are partway through a larger study of what happens after a booking is made, and I will share the full picture when it is ready. One early pattern is worth mentioning now: hosts with no buffers move meetings more often, and a meeting the host moves is missed more often than one that stays where it was booked. The buffer is not just protecting your afternoon. It is protecting the booking.

## How to set it in Openslot

Open the booking page you want to change, then Availability, then Buffers. Set the time before and after separately. Daily limit and minimum notice are on the same screen, under Limits.

The settings are per booking page, so a 15-minute buffer after a demo does not have to apply to a 15-minute intro call. Set the rules for each kind of meeting you take, and keep the stricter rules on the meetings that matter most.

## Checklist

- Add a buffer of at least 10 minutes after every event type.
- Add a buffer before any meeting you need to prepare for.
- Set a daily limit lower than you think you need, and raise it only if a month goes by without regret.
- Require enough notice that a booking never surprises you.
- Review the settings per booking page, not once for everything.
